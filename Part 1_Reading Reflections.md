## 1.1 Intro to SwE

**Tar Pit Woes**

Mr. Stride identifies them as:

1. Computers require perfection  
2. Other people’s requirements are hard to know  
3. Other people's work can be faulty  
4. Debugging is tedious. Finding bugs is work\!  
5. Testing drags on and on.  
6. Product is obsolete before it is done

**Right on Schedule**  
One could infer that Software Engineering is a balance between Meeting a commitment and Managing Risks.  
More directly it states that there are two types of Scheduling/Project Management: “Meeting a commitment” and “Managing risk”  
Instead of informing their managers of the delay, **they avoid facing conflict**, \[Stride: and in order to avoid facing conflict, they\] rush the work, sacrifice quality, and slip the schedule, all with little control or visibility. It’s the opposite of what managers should want, yet those same managers insist on following the schedule precisely. Why? Because most managers and engineers don’t distinguish between the two types of scheduling—meeting a commitment and managing risk  
Question: how can you “follow the schedule precisely” and “slip the schedule” at the same time?

The article also states:  
While many software projects require little or no coordination across groups, running large and mature projects typically involves meeting commitments, and thus formal project management techniques.

Scrum is an excellent example of software development practices that focus on risk management.

To help them make their commitments, companies typically use inventory, buffers, and other forms of risk management for individual steps in the process. That’s the whole point. You use **formal project management** for meeting your high-level commitments and **risk management** for properly completing the individual steps in your process. **Risk management is about making sure the critical work gets done properly, even in highly variable environments.**

## 1.2 Essentials, Errors, SDLCs

Here are answers for each question for "No Silver Bullet".

**1a. Main point of the article** 

Brooks argues that there is no single technique or technology that will produce an order-of-magnitude improvement in software productivity, reliability, or simplicity within a decade, because the hardest parts of software development are conceptual, not mechanical; there is no "silver bullet"

**1b. What are the Essential Difficulties?** 

The essential difficulties are **inherent** to the nature of software itself: **complexity** (no two parts are alike, and systems grow non-linearly), **conformity** (software must conform to messy external interfaces, hardware, and human institutions), **changeability** (software is under constant pressure to change because it is the "soft" part of any system), and **invisibility** (software has no natural geometric representation, making it hard to visualize or communicate). These are difficulties in the essence of the problem, so no tool can remove them.

**1c. What are the Accidental Difficulties and how do they relate to the Essential Difficulties?** 

Accidental difficulties are the problems that arise during the effort of building software. They come from the tools, languages, techniques and processes we use like slow compile times, clumsy notations, or primitive debugging. These do not arise from from the "essential problem" itself. Brooks says past "silver bullets" (high-level languages, time-sharing, unified environments) succeeded precisely because they attacked these accidental difficulties; but since most of that low-hanging fruit has already been picked, further tooling improvements can no longer deliver the same order-of-magnitude gains, leaving the essential difficulties as the dominant remaining cost.

**1d. Has AI changed anything?** 

This one is meant to be open-ended for discussion, but here is what one might say:  

AI coding assistants have continued to reduced some accidental difficulties, and some of them have been reduced significantly (boilerplate, syntax lookup, translating intent into code). But they have not eliminated the essential difficulties Brooks identified. Someone still has to specify what the system should actually do, resolve conflicting stakeholder requirements, and manage a design's complexity and conformity to the real world. The case can be made that AI is another productivity multiplier on the "accidental" side.

Modern AI coding tools are genuinely powerful. They can generate a working function from a description, convert code between languages, write repetitive test scaffolding, or explain an unfamiliar API in seconds. These were the kind of grunt work that used to eat up hours. In that sense AI behaves exactly like the high-level languages, IDEs, and version control systems Brooks credited with earlier productivity gains: AI removes friction from *expressing* a solution. 

AI raises the ceiling on how fast the accidental work gets done, but the bottleneck Brooks was actually worried about is, arguably, still squarely a human, essential-difficulty problem. 

**Death March**  
A Death March is when a product team works incredibly long, **\*\*forced\*\*** **hours** over an “extended” period of time to ship a product.   
Managers do them because they think it’ll work for them and they see no alternatives.

* Management is remarkably stupid: “too much work to do? Work harder\!”  
* Management is incredibly naive: Adding more hours will increase productivity.  
* Management is tragically foolish: “My team will beat the odds\!”  
* Management is unconscionably irresponsible: A death march destroys a team.  
* Management is unaccountably spineless: **Lack the courage to say “no”.**

> **Side note**: Courage is one of the 5 Values of Extreme Programming

Managers can reap short-term rewards from a successful Death March: a big product shipped on an aggressive schedule against all odds. Sometimes this manager will move onto a new team to repeat their “success” before the long-term impacts come to bear: demoralized team, unsatisfied customers, extensive maintenance, difficult DevOps, v-next is slow due to technical debt.

**What are the signs?**

* Low morale (but sometimes, a successful march can create a sense of victory. People often enjoy challenges, even grueling ones, if they triumph and feel rewarded.)  
* Long hours **required**, even over productivity metrics

**How related to SDLC? ** 
A Death March is a painful consequence resulting from a failed plan. SDLC is about planning and executing in ways to avoid a Death March. 

Alternatives:  
Management needs to **plan for the worst case** by: having a solid MVP, reacting early and swiftly to small failures, and make the hard calls (e.g. cutting features, slipping dates, adding resources & costs, tough conversations with customers)

## 1.4 SDLC Survey Set 2

**Prompt:** Let's recognize that all SDLCs are in one way or another a hybrid of techniques, and SDLCs belong on a spectrum from Planned to Agile. How can we describe the SDLC's progression? Waterfall to V-Shaped to Spiral to Evolutionary Prototyping? 

We can progress from Waterfall to V-Shaped to Spiral to Evolutionary Prototyping. Each model gets us closer Agile.

V-Shaped is like waterfall, but development is closely tied to testing early on. (It is almost like Test Driven Development.)  
Spiral then adds iterations and incrementally builds something that is still nicely tested, focusing on reducing risk. The product ships at the end.  
Evolutionary prototyping then adds the creation of a prototype with each iteration improving the prototype that is given to and evaluated by the customer. (The final product is the final prototype.)

Loosely speaking, each model bolts on one (or more) new capability while carrying forward the previous one's strengths. 

Waterfall → **\+ early test alignment** → V-Shaped   
V-Shaped → **\+ iterative risk-driven loops** → Spiral   
Spiral → **\+ customer-evaluated evolving prototypes each loop** → Evolutionary Prototyping

## 1.5 Agile & Scrum

**1\. What makes an SDLC Agile?**

An SDLC is Agile when it embraces iterative, incremental development with frequent feedback loops, allowing plans and requirements to adapt as the project progresses rather than being fixed upfront. It prioritizes working software, collaboration, and responsiveness to change over rigid adherence to an initial plan. An Agile methodology anticipates change and strives to make the cost of changes inexpensive because they're expected.

**2\. Without listing the 4 Values and 12 Principles, what are they?**

The 4 Values are high level statements of priority that establish what Agile practitioners **should value more** when tensions arise between two competing approaches. 

The 12 Principles are more specific, actionable guidelines that describe how teams **should behave and operate** in order to live out those values day to day.

**3\. What distinguishes the 4 Values from the 12 Principles?**

The **Values** are broad philosophical priorities *expressed as tradeoffs*, 

The **Principles** are concrete, practical guidance that translate those priorities into *observable practices and team behaviors*. 

In short, the ***Values describe a mindset and the Principles describe how that mindset shows up in action***.

#### How does Agile differ from a traditional plan-driven SDLC such as Waterfall…  

1. **How does Agile differ from a traditional plan-driven SDLC such as Waterfall when *requirements change during a project*?**

Agile expects and **welcomes** changing requirements even late in development, adjusting scope in short cycles based on new information or feedback.

Waterfall treats requirements as fixed after the initial phase, so changes typically require formal change control processes and **can be costly or disruptive**.

2. **How does Agile differ from a traditional plan-driven SDLC such as Waterfall in *how communication is executed*?**

Agile favors frequent, informal, **face to face or synchronous communication** such as daily standups and regular check-ins throughout the project. 

Waterfall relies more heavily on **formal documentation** and scheduled milestone reviews, with less continuous back and forth between phases.

3. **How does Agile differ from a traditional plan-driven SDLC such as Waterfall in *how progress is measured and evaluated*?**

Agile measures progress through **working software delivered** in short iterations, often using metrics like velocity or completed increments. 

Waterfall measures progress by **completion of predefined phases** and documentation milestones, with actual working software typically not evaluated until much later.

4. **How does Agile differ from a traditional plan-driven SDLC such as Waterfall in *how customers and stakeholders are involved*?**

Agile involves customers and stakeholders **continuously**, gathering feedback after each iteration to guide subsequent work. 

Waterfall typically involves stakeholders **heavily at the start** to define requirements and again at the end for delivery, with minimal engagement during the development phases in between.
